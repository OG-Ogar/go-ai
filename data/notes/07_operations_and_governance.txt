SECURITY OPERATIONS AND INCIDENT RESPONSE

THE SECURITY OPERATIONS CENTER

A security operations center monitors, detects, investigates, and responds to
security events. Roles include tier one triage, tier two investigation, tier
three threat hunting and engineering, and an incident commander for major
events.

A typical shift reviews alerts from endpoint, network, identity, and cloud
telemetry. Analysts decide whether an alert is a true positive, then contain
and escalate as needed.

SOC metrics include alert volume, false positive rate, time to detect, time
to respond, and coverage of critical assets. Too many untuned alerts cause
fatigue, which is itself a security risk.

ALERT TUNING AND DETECTION ENGINEERING

Detection engineering writes and maintains detection rules. A good rule has a
clear hypothesis, known false positives, a severity, and a response guide.
Rules should be tested against real and synthetic data.

Sigma is a vendor-neutral format for detection rules. YARA matches patterns
in files. Suricata and Snort rules match network traffic.

Detection coverage is often mapped to MITRE ATT&CK techniques so that gaps are
visible.

THREAT HUNTING

Threat hunting is a proactive search for hidden compromise that alerts did not
catch. A hunt starts with a hypothesis, such as an attacker using remote
management tools, then queries telemetry for evidence. Hunts produce new
detections and improved visibility.

INCIDENT RESPONSE

The NIST incident response life cycle has four phases: preparation; detection
and analysis; containment, eradication, and recovery; and post-incident
activity.

Preparation includes plans, roles, contacts, tooling, backups, and exercises.
A team should know who declares an incident and who communicates externally.

Detection and analysis confirms whether an event is an incident, determines
scope, and preserves evidence.

Containment stops the spread. Short-term containment might isolate a host.
Long-term containment might rebuild a segment. Eradication removes the cause,
such as malware, backdoors, and vulnerable software. Recovery restores normal
operations and monitors for recurrence.

Post-incident activity documents what happened, what worked, and what to
change. A blameless review improves systems rather than punishing individuals.

Incident response playbooks define step-by-step actions for common scenarios
such as ransomware, business email compromise, data loss, and insider threat.
Tabletop exercises test the playbook before a real crisis.

EVIDENCE HANDLING AND CHAIN OF CUSTODY

Evidence must be collected in a way that preserves integrity and is
admissible if legal action follows. Document who collected what, when, where,
and how. Use write blockers when imaging disks. Hash images and record the
hash. Store evidence in a controlled location with limited access.

Order of volatility guides collection: capture memory and network state before
powering down, because volatile data disappears.

DIGITAL FORENSICS

Disk forensics examines file systems, deleted files, and metadata. Memory
forensics examines running processes, network connections, and injected code.
Network forensics examines packet captures and flow logs. Log forensics
reconstructs activity across systems.

File system artifacts include timestamps, prefetch files, shell bags, jump
lists, and recycle bin contents. Windows event logs record logons, process
creation, and service installation. The Windows registry stores configuration
and persistence keys.

On Linux, useful artifacts include authentication logs, shell history,
systemd units, cron jobs, and package manager logs.

A timeline correlates events across sources to reconstruct an attack.

Anti-forensics includes timestomping, log deletion, and encryption. Defenders
counter it with centralized logging and integrity monitoring.

VULNERABILITY MANAGEMENT

Vulnerability management is a continuous cycle: inventory assets, scan for
weaknesses, prioritize, remediate, and verify. Scanning without remediation is
wasted effort.

Risk-based prioritization considers exploitability, exposure, business
criticality, and available threat intelligence. The common vulnerability
scoring system gives a severity score from zero to ten, but severity alone is
not priority. The exploit prediction scoring system estimates the likelihood
that a vulnerability will be exploited. Known exploited vulnerability catalogs
list flaws actively used in attacks.

Patch management must include third-party software, firmware, and container
images, not just the operating system.

Compensating controls buy time when patching is impossible: network
isolation, virtual patching with a web application firewall, and increased
monitoring.

Penetration testing validates defenses through authorized simulated attacks.
It has phases: scoping and rules of engagement, reconnaissance, exploitation,
post-exploitation, reporting, and retesting. A test without written
authorization is a crime, not a test.

Red teams simulate realistic adversaries over longer periods. Blue teams
defend. Purple teams improve collaboration by turning red findings into blue
detections.

Bug bounty programs invite external researchers to report vulnerabilities for
rewards. They require clear scope, safe harbor language, and triage capacity.

RISK MANAGEMENT

Qualitative risk uses categories such as low, medium, and high. Quantitative
risk assigns money. Single loss expectancy is the cost of one event. Annualized
rate of occurrence is expected events per year. Annualized loss expectancy is
single loss expectancy multiplied by annualized rate of occurrence. Return on
security investment compares loss avoided with control cost.

Risk responses are mitigate, transfer, avoid, and accept. Insurance transfers
financial risk. Accepting risk must be documented and owned.

A risk register lists risks, owners, likelihood, impact, controls, and
treatment plans.

FRAMEWORKS AND COMPLIANCE

The NIST cybersecurity framework organizes outcomes into identify, protect,
detect, respond, recover, and govern. It is voluntary and widely used.

ISO 27001 specifies requirements for an information security management
system: leadership, risk assessment, controls, and continual improvement. ISO
27002 provides control guidance.

The CIS critical security controls are a prioritized set of technical
safeguards, including inventory, configuration, access control, logging, and
malware defense.

COBIT and ITIL focus on governance and service management.

Compliance regimes map to legal obligations. The general data protection
regulation protects personal data of European residents and requires lawful
basis, minimization, breach notification, and privacy by design. The health
insurance portability and accountability act protects health information in
the United States. The payment card industry data security standard protects
cardholder data. SOC 2 reports on trust services criteria for service
organizations. Sarbanes-Oxley requires financial controls for public companies.

Compliance is a floor, not a ceiling. An organization can be compliant and
still insecure.

PRIVACY AND DATA PROTECTION

Privacy principles include lawfulness, purpose limitation, data minimization,
accuracy, storage limitation, integrity, and accountability.

Data loss prevention detects and blocks sensitive data leaving controlled
environments. It uses pattern matching, fingerprinting, and classification
labels. It is most effective when combined with clear policy and user
education.

Data retention schedules define how long data is kept and when it is
destroyed. Secure destruction uses shredding for paper and cryptographic
erasure or overwriting for media.

BUSINESS CONTINUITY AND DISASTER RECOVERY

Business continuity keeps essential functions running during disruption.
Disaster recovery restores technology after a major failure. Key metrics are
recovery time objective, the maximum acceptable downtime, and recovery point
objective, the maximum acceptable data loss.

Backups must be tested. The three-two-one rule suggests three copies, on two
media types, with one offsite. Immutable backups resist ransomware.

Failover sites may be hot, warm, or cold depending on readiness and cost.

SECURITY AWARENESS AND CULTURE

Awareness programs teach employees to recognize phishing, protect
credentials, handle data correctly, and report incidents quickly. Training
should be short, frequent, and relevant to the person's role.

Simulated phishing measures susceptibility but must be handled carefully to
avoid eroding trust. The goal is a culture where reporting is safe and valued.

Leadership sets the tone. Security policies are useless if executives ignore
them.

LEGAL AND ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

Security work must be authorized and lawful. Testing systems without
permission violates computer misuse laws. Handling personal data requires a
legal basis. Evidence must be preserved properly if prosecution is possible.

Ethical duties include minimizing harm, protecting confidentiality of
findings, disclosing vulnerabilities responsibly, and avoiding surveillance
that is disproportionate to the risk.

Responsible disclosure gives vendors time to fix a flaw before public
details are released. Coordinated disclosure involves a facilitator when
parties disagree.
