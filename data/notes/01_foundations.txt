SECURITY FOUNDATIONS

THE CIA TRIAD

The CIA triad is the foundation of information security. It names the three
properties that security work tries to protect.

Confidentiality means that information is only readable by people who are
authorized to read it. Encryption, access control lists, file permissions,
and data classification all exist to protect confidentiality. A breach of
confidentiality is called a disclosure.

Integrity means that information is accurate and has not been changed by
anyone who is not allowed to change it. Hashing, digital signatures, version
control, and write permissions protect integrity. A breach of integrity is
called an alteration, and it is often more dangerous than disclosure because
decisions are made on corrupted data.

Availability means that systems and data are reachable when authorized users
need them. Backups, redundancy, load balancing, and denial of service
protection protect availability. A breach of availability is called a
destruction or an interruption.

A common extension is the Parkerian hexad, which adds possession or control,
authenticity, and utility. Another extension adds non-repudiation, meaning a
person cannot credibly deny having performed an action, which is achieved
with digital signatures and audit logs.

THE AAA MODEL

Authentication answers the question: who are you? It is proven with
something you know, such as a password; something you have, such as a token
or phone; something you are, such as a fingerprint; somewhere you are, such
as a network location; or something you do, such as a typing rhythm.

Authorization answers the question: what are you allowed to do? It is
enforced by permissions, roles, and policy.

Accounting, also called auditing, answers the question: what did you do?
It is recorded in logs that must be protected from tampering.

Multi-factor authentication requires two or more different categories.
A password plus a one-time code is multi-factor. Two passwords are not,
because they are both something you know.

THREAT, VULNERABILITY, RISK, AND EXPLOIT

A threat is a potential cause of harm, such as an attacker, a flood, or a
bug. A vulnerability is a weakness that a threat can use, such as an
unpatched server or a careless employee. An exploit is the concrete technique
or code that abuses a vulnerability. Risk is the combination of likelihood
and impact. Risk equals threat multiplied by vulnerability multiplied by
impact, in the simplest qualitative model.

Security is not the same as zero risk. Security is the management of risk
down to an acceptable level at an acceptable cost. Controls reduce either the
likelihood of an event or its impact.

TYPES OF SECURITY CONTROLS

Preventive controls stop an incident before it happens. Examples include
firewalls, locks, input validation, and security awareness training.

Detective controls notice an incident while or after it happens. Examples
include intrusion detection systems, log monitoring, and file integrity
monitoring.

Corrective controls limit damage and restore service after an incident.
Examples include backups, incident response playbooks, and failover systems.

Deterrent controls discourage attackers. Warning banners, visible cameras,
and legal notices are deterrents.

Compensating controls are alternatives used when the primary control cannot
be applied, such as a network segmentation rule when a host cannot be patched.

Controls are also classified as administrative, such as policies and
training; technical, such as software and hardware; and physical, such as
guards, fences, and locks.

DEFENSE IN DEPTH

Defense in depth means layering independent controls so that the failure of
one does not cause a total compromise. A typical layered architecture has
perimeter firewalls, network segmentation, host firewalls, endpoint
detection, application authentication, encryption, monitoring, and backups.
An attacker must defeat many layers, which raises cost and creates chances to
detect them.

LEAST PRIVILEGE AND NEED TO KNOW

Least privilege means every user, process, and service receives only the
permissions required for its job, and only for as long as required. Need to
know is the data-specific version of the same idea. These principles limit
the blast radius of a compromised account.

SEPARATION OF DUTIES

Separation of duties splits a sensitive process between multiple people so
that no single person can abuse it alone. The classic example is that the
person who requests a payment is not the person who approves it. In
cybersecurity, the person who writes code should not be the only person who
can deploy it to production.

SECURE BY DESIGN AND ZERO TRUST

Secure by design means building security into a system from the beginning
rather than bolting it on later. It includes threat modeling, secure
defaults, minimal attack surface, and failing safely.

Zero trust is a model that removes the assumption that anything inside the
network is trustworthy. Every request is authenticated, authorized, and
encrypted regardless of network location. The slogan is never trust, always
verify. Practical zero trust uses strong identity, device health checks,
micro-segmentation, and continuous monitoring.

THE ATTACK SURFACE

The attack surface is the sum of all points where an attacker could try to
enter or extract data. It includes exposed network services, web forms,
APIs, user accounts, supply chain dependencies, physical ports, and even
employees through social engineering. Reducing the attack surface means
removing unnecessary services, closing unused ports, disabling unused
accounts, and minimizing installed software.

ASSETS, DATA CLASSIFICATION, AND THE CROWN JEWELS

An asset is anything of value: data, systems, people, reputation, or
facilities. Data classification labels information by sensitivity, for
example public, internal, confidential, and restricted. Classification
drives how data is stored, shared, logged, and destroyed. Crown jewels are
the assets whose loss would hurt the organization most, such as customer
databases, source code, or signing keys. Defenses should be strongest around
crown jewels.

THE SECURITY LIFE CYCLE

Security is continuous, not a one-time project. The cycle is: identify assets
and risks; protect with controls; detect problems; respond to incidents; and
recover and improve. Frameworks such as NIST Cybersecurity Framework express
this as identify, protect, detect, respond, and recover, with governance as
an organizing function.

THREAT MODELING

Threat modeling is a structured way to find design weaknesses before
attackers do. A simple method is to ask four questions: what are we building;
what can go wrong; what are we going to do about it; and did we do a good
job. STRIDE is a popular checklist: spoofing, tampering, repudiation,
information disclosure, denial of service, and elevation of privilege. Each
component of a system, its trust boundaries, and the data flows between them
should be examined.

COMMON SECURITY METRICS

Mean time to detect measures how long it takes to notice an intrusion.
Mean time to respond measures how long it takes to contain it. Patch
latency measures the gap between a vendor fix and deployment. Phishing
click rate measures user susceptibility. Coverage metrics measure the
percentage of assets with logging, endpoint agents, or backups. Metrics are
useful only when tied to decisions.
