THREATS AND THREAT ACTORS

WHY ATTACKERS ATTACK

Attackers are motivated by money, espionage, ideology, revenge, curiosity,
status, or coercion. Motivation predicts behavior. A financially motivated
criminal wants fast access to payment data and will often leave as soon as
they are paid. A state-sponsored group wants long-term persistence and
secrecy. An insider may want to leak data or sabotage systems they already
legitimately access.

THREAT ACTOR CATEGORIES

Script kiddies use existing tools without deep understanding. They are noisy
and easy to detect but can still cause damage.

Hacktivists attack for political or social reasons. They favor website
defacement, data leaks, and denial of service.

Cybercriminals operate for profit and often run industrialized operations
such as ransomware-as-a-service, phishing kits, and credential markets.

Insider threats are current or former employees, contractors, or partners.
They may act maliciously, or they may be negligent, or their account may be
compromised by an outsider.

Nation-state actors have the most resources and patience. They conduct
espionage, intellectual property theft, disruption, and prepositioning for
future conflict. Advanced persistent threat is the term for a long-term,
stealthy, well-resourced intrusion campaign.

Competitors may conduct corporate espionage, though this overlaps with
criminal and state activity.

Hackers are also described by hat color. White hat hackers work with
authorization and report findings. Black hat hackers act illegally. Grey hat
hackers act without authorization but often without malicious intent.

THE CYBER KILL CHAIN

The kill chain describes the stages of an intrusion, adapted from military
targeting. Reconnaissance gathers information about the target. Weaponization
builds a deliverable such as a malicious document. Delivery sends it, for
example by email. Exploitation triggers the vulnerability. Installation
establishes persistence, such as a backdoor. Command and control connects the
implant to the attacker. Actions on objectives achieve the goal, such as data
theft or encryption. Defenders can break the chain at any stage.

MITRE ATT&CK

MITRE ATT&CK is a knowledge base of real attacker behaviors organized into
tactics, techniques, and procedures. Tactics are the why, such as initial
access or persistence. Techniques are the how, such as phishing or
scheduled tasks. Procedures are the specific implementations observed in the
wild. Defenders use ATT&CK to map detections, plan threat hunting, and
measure coverage.

THE DIAMOND MODEL

The diamond model analyzes an intrusion along four vertices: adversary,
capability, infrastructure, and victim. Each event connects these four
elements. The model helps analysts pivot, for example from one malicious IP
address to other victims that talked to it.

THE ATTACK LIFE CYCLE

A practical life cycle has these phases. Reconnaissance studies the target.
Initial access gains a foothold. Execution runs code. Persistence survives
reboots. Privilege escalation gains higher rights. Defense evasion avoids
detection. Credential access steals passwords or tokens. Discovery maps the
environment. Lateral movement reaches other systems. Collection gathers data.
Exfiltration removes it. Impact destroys or extorts.

RECONNAISSANCE

Passive reconnaissance collects information without touching the target
directly. Examples include search engines, social media, certificate
transparency logs, DNS records, job postings, and public code repositories.

Active reconnaissance sends traffic to the target. Examples include port
scanning, service fingerprinting, directory brute forcing, and vulnerability
scanning. Active reconnaissance is louder and more likely to be logged.

OSINT, or open source intelligence, is the discipline of gathering
information from public sources. Attackers use it to find employee names,
email formats, technologies in use, and exposed credentials.

SCANNING AND ENUMERATION

A port scan asks which TCP or UDP ports on a host accept connections. A
synchronize scan sends a TCP SYN and never completes the handshake. A connect
scan completes the handshake and is easier to log. Service detection sends
probes and compares responses to signatures to guess software and version.

Enumeration extracts details such as usernames, shares, groups, and
software versions. Tools include Nmap for networks, Gobuster and ffuf for web
content, and BloodHound for Active Directory relationships.

VULNERABILITY SCANNING

A vulnerability scanner compares a system against a database of known
weaknesses. It reports findings with severity scores. Scanners produce false
positives and false negatives, so findings must be validated. Scanning
without authorization is unethical and often illegal.

ZERO-DAY AND N-DAY

A zero-day vulnerability is unknown to the vendor, so no patch exists. An
n-day vulnerability is known and patched, but many systems remain unpatched.
Most real-world compromise uses n-days, because patching is slow.

EXPLOIT KITS AND WEAPONIZATION

An exploit kit is a service that packages exploits for browser and plugin
flaws behind a malicious web page. It fingerprints the visitor and chooses a
suitable exploit. Weaponization also includes malicious documents, macro
payloads, and trojanized installers.

ADVANCED PERSISTENT THREAT BEHAVIOR

APTs favor living off the land, meaning they use legitimate tools already
present on the system, such as PowerShell, Windows Management
Instrumentation, and scheduled tasks. This blends with normal administration
and frustrates signature-based detection. They move slowly, use encrypted
channels, and maintain multiple fallback access paths.

THREAT INTELLIGENCE

Threat intelligence is evidence-based knowledge about adversaries. Strategic
intelligence informs leadership. Operational intelligence informs campaigns.
Tactical intelligence informs attacker tactics. Technical intelligence
provides indicators such as IP addresses, domains, file hashes, and
mutexes. Indicators age quickly, so context matters more than a list of
blocked addresses.

The intelligence cycle is planning, collection, processing, analysis,
dissemination, and feedback. A common standard for sharing is STIX for
structured data and TAXII for transport. Open sources include vendor blogs,
government advisories, and information sharing and analysis centers.

INSIDER THREAT INDICATORS

Warning signs include access attempts outside normal hours, large downloads,
use of unauthorized storage devices, attempts to bypass controls, sudden
interest in unrelated systems, and resignation combined with unusual data
access. Technical controls include data loss prevention, user behavior
analytics, and least privilege. Cultural controls include fair treatment and
clear reporting channels.
